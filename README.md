•	Carbon–carbon, steel, or titanium structure.

2. Operating story (short, patent style)
1.	Plasma-facing CPS operation
o	The tungsten CPS mesh is continuously wetted with Li–Sn from the plenum.
o	Capillary forces retain a thin, stable liquid film.
o	Lithium segregates to the surface, forming a low Z plasma interface.
2.	Heat transfer path
o	Plasma dumps heat into the Li–Sn film and CPS mesh.
o	Heat conducts through the CPS and the separating wall into the Li–Pb–Sn bulk loop.
o	Li–Sn flow can be very slow—its job is surface chemistry and local cooling, not bulk transport.
3.	Bulk loop and electromagnetic drive
o	The Li–Pb–Sn loop circulates in a closed path behind the wall.
o	Electromagnetic coils (Halbach like phased stator) generate a traveling field that pushes the conductive Li–Pb–Sn along the channel.
o	This loop removes most of the thermal power and provides neutron shielding.
4.	Safety and contamination control
o	Pb and most Sn are confined to the rear loop and never contact the plasma.
o	If the CPS layer is damaged, the first exposed fluid is still Li–Sn, not Li–Pb–Sn.
o	Diagnostics watch for high Z lines; if detected, flow and power are adjusted or the system ramps down.

PART 2 — PATENT READY “DETAILED DESCRIPTION”

Detailed Description of the Preferred Embodiments
The present disclosure describes a dual alloy liquid metal divertor assembly configured to provide a low atomic number plasma facing interface while simultaneously enabling high capacity thermal removal and neutron shielding through a magnetohydrodynamically driven bulk coolant loop.
1. Plasma Facing Capillary Porous Structure
A plasma facing capillary porous structure (CPS) is positioned adjacent to the divertor strike zone. The CPS comprises a refractory metal mesh, such as tungsten or tungsten zirconium carbide, having a thickness between 0.5 mm and 1.0 mm and a porosity between 60% and 80%.
The CPS is wetted with a binary lithium–tin alloy. Capillary forces retain the alloy within the mesh, forming a stable liquid film at the plasma interface. Thermodynamic surface segregation causes lithium to preferentially occupy the outermost atomic layer, thereby presenting a low atomic number plasma facing surface while tin remains confined within the porous structure.
2. Lithium–Tin Plenum Layer
A shallow plenum is disposed immediately behind the CPS. The plenum is fluidly coupled to a lithium–tin supply manifold and maintains a laminar replenishment flow of 0.1–0.5 m/s. The plenum ensures continuous wetting of the CPS and isolates the plasma facing alloy from the bulk coolant loop.
3. Thermal Interface Barrier
A solid thermal interface wall is positioned behind the plenum. The wall comprises silicon carbide, tungsten coated silicon carbide, or another chemically inert, thermally conductive material. The wall prevents mixing between the lithium–tin alloy and the bulk coolant while conducting heat into the rear channel.
4. Bulk Coolant Loop
A bulk coolant channel is located behind the thermal interface wall. The channel contains a ternary lithium–lead–tin alloy selected for high density, high heat capacity, and neutron moderating properties. The bulk alloy circulates at a velocity between 1.0 m/s and 3.0 m/s and removes the majority of the thermal load from the divertor region.
5. Electromagnetic Propulsion System
An electromagnetic propulsion system is arranged adjacent to the bulk coolant channel. The propulsion system comprises a phased coil array configured to generate a traveling magnetic field along the channel. The traveling field induces Lorentz forces within the conductive lithium–lead–tin alloy, thereby driving the bulk coolant in a controlled, low velocity circulation path.
The CPS layer remains unaffected by the propulsion system due to the presence of the thermal interface wall and the low electrical conductivity of the lithium–tin alloy at the plasma facing surface.
6. System Advantages
The dual alloy configuration provides:
•	a low Z lithium plasma interface,
•	a capillary stabilized liquid film resistant to detachment,
•	high capacity thermal removal via the bulk loop,
•	neutron shielding from the lead containing alloy,
•	and electromagnetic pumping isolated from the plasma facing region.

3. FMEA – key failure modes and effects
Failure mode 1 – CPS dry out (loss of Li–Sn wetting)
•	Cause: insufficient Li–Sn supply, blockage, overheating.
•	Effect: tungsten CPS exposed directly to plasma → rapid erosion, high Z W in plasma.
•	Mitigation:
o	flow sensors in Li–Sn plenum,
o	temperature sensors in CPS,
o	automatic plasma power ramp down if CPS temperature exceeds threshold.
Failure mode 2 – High Z contamination (Pb/Sn incursion)
•	Cause: breach in thermal interface wall, mixing of Li–Pb–Sn into Li–Sn plenum.
•	Effect: tin/lead reach plasma → strong radiative cooling → plasma collapse.
•	Mitigation:
o	double wall thermal barrier,
o	leak detection via composition sensors,
o	fast plasma shutdown if high Z spectral lines detected.
Failure mode 3 – Bulk loop stagnation
•	Cause: electromagnetic drive failure, power loss, coil fault.
•	Effect: Li–Pb–Sn stops moving → overheating of bulk loop and interface wall.
•	Mitigation:
o	flow meters in bulk loop,
o	automatic reduction of plasma power,
o	emergency coolant bypass or passive drain tank.
Failure mode 4 – Overheating of CPS
•	Cause: ELMs or transient spikes exceeding design heat flux.
•	Effect: excessive Li evaporation, CPS damage.
•	Mitigation:
o	passive vapor shielding (lithium vapor cloud),
o	active ELM control in plasma,
o	divertor power handling limits enforced by control system.
Failure mode 5 – Coil overheating or quench
•	Cause: excessive current, cooling failure.
•	Effect: loss of pumping, possible mechanical stress.
•	Mitigation:
o	temperature and current monitoring,
o	staged ramp down of drive,
o	redundant cooling paths.

6. Physics justification section 
•	Low Z plasma interface: Lithium at the surface minimizes radiative losses and impurity driven cooling, enabling higher edge temperatures and improved divertor performance.
•	Capillary stabilization: The CPS mesh uses surface tension and pore geometry to retain a thin liquid film against gravity, MHD forces, and moderate plasma transients, reducing droplet ejection and splashing.
•	Dual alloy separation: Using Li–Sn at the interface and Li–Pb–Sn in the bulk loop decouples plasma compatibility from thermal and neutron performance. High Z elements (Pb, most Sn) are confined to regions that never contact the plasma.
•	Electromagnetic pumping in the bulk only: The electromagnetic drive acts exclusively on the rear loop, where strong MHD forces are beneficial for pumping and do not disturb the plasma. The CPS and Li–Sn plenum remain hydrodynamically and electromagnetically quiet.
•	Neutron and thermal management: The Li–Pb–Sn loop provides high density and heat capacity, improving neutron shielding and heat removal without compromising the plasma facing surface.
•	Passive safety features: Lithium evaporation under extreme heat provides a vapor shielding effect, spreading localized heat loads and reducing peak surface temperatures. The CPS geometry promotes rapid re wetting after transient events.

Detailed description of the preferred embodiments
The present disclosure relates to a dual alloy liquid metal divertor assembly for magnetic confinement fusion reactors. The assembly is configured to provide a low atomic number plasma facing interface while simultaneously enabling high capacity thermal removal and neutron shielding via a magnetohydrodynamically driven bulk coolant loop. The design separates plasma compatibility, heat exhaust, and neutron management into distinct functional layers, each optimized for its specific role.
Plasma-facing capillary porous structure (CPS)
Construction: The plasma-facing element is a capillary porous structure positioned adjacent to the divertor strike zone and scrape-off layer. The CPS comprises a refractory metal mesh, such as porous tungsten or tungsten zirconium carbide (W–ZrC), with a thickness between 0.5 mm and 1.0 mm, porosity between 60% and 80%, and pore sizes in the range of 10–50 µm.
“A plasma facing capillary porous structure (CPS) is positioned adjacent to the divertor strike zone. The CPS comprises a refractory metal mesh, such as tungsten or tungsten zirconium carbide, having a thickness between 0.5 mm and 1.0 mm and a porosity between 60% and 80%.”
Fluid and surface chemistry: The CPS is continuously wetted with a binary lithium–tin alloy, for example a eutectic Li₂₅Sn₇₅ composition. Capillary forces retain the alloy within the mesh, forming a thin, stable liquid film at the plasma interface. Thermodynamic Gibbs segregation causes lithium to preferentially occupy the outermost atomic layer, presenting an effectively low Z (Z=3) plasma facing surface, while tin remains buried within the porous network. This configuration minimizes radiative losses and impurity driven cooling and provides a self healing liquid surface that can re wet after transient events.
Lithium–tin plenum layer
Geometry and function: Immediately behind the CPS is a shallow lithium–tin plenum, formed as a cavity of approximately 2–5 mm thickness. This plenum is fluidly coupled to external Li–Sn supply and return manifolds and maintains a slow, laminar replenishment flow in the range of 0.1–0.5 m/s.
“A shallow plenum is disposed immediately behind the CPS. The plenum is fluidly coupled to a lithium–tin supply manifold and maintains a laminar replenishment flow of 0.1–0.5 m/s.”
The plenum ensures continuous wetting of the CPS, isolates the plasma facing alloy from the rear bulk coolant loop, and buffers the CPS from turbulence or pressure fluctuations. Its low pressure, laminar regime prevents jetting or bubbling at the plasma surface and allows controlled replenishment of lithium–tin without disturbing the capillary stabilized film.
Thermal interface barrier (SiC/carbide wall)
Material and role: Behind the Li–Sn plenum is a solid thermal interface wall that separates the front CPS/plenum region from the rear bulk coolant channel. The wall comprises silicon carbide (SiC), tungsten-coated SiC, or a hybrid SiC/carbide stack, with a typical thickness of 2–4 mm. In some embodiments, a transition carbide layer (e.g., WC or TiC, 50–200 µm) is applied between the tungsten CPS and SiC to improve chemical compatibility and share thermal stresses, and an optional rear carbide liner (20–100 µm) is applied on the bulk side to protect SiC from Li–Pb–Sn attack.
“A solid thermal interface wall is positioned behind the plenum. The wall comprises silicon carbide, tungsten coated silicon carbide, or another chemically inert, thermally conductive material. The wall prevents mixing between the lithium–tin alloy and the bulk coolant while conducting heat into the rear channel.”
Functions:
•	Conducts heat from the CPS and Li–Sn plenum into the bulk coolant channel.
•	Prevents direct mixing of Li–Sn and Li–Pb–Sn, confining high Z elements (Pb and most Sn) to regions that never contact the plasma.
•	Provides structural support and electrical insulation, reducing parasitic MHD currents in the wall.
•	Shares mechanical and chemical loads across multiple layers so no single material bears all stresses.
Bulk coolant loop (Li–Pb–Sn channel)
Fluid and geometry: Behind the thermal interface barrier lies a deeper bulk coolant channel, typically 20–40 mm in cross-sectional depth, containing a ternary lithium–lead–tin alloy. It absorbs heat conducted through the SiC/carbide barrier, transports it to external heat exchangers, and provides neutron shielding for the divertor cassette and surrounding structures. The bulk channel walls—SiC/SiC composite or W-lined Mo/steel—contain the hydrostatic and dynamic pressure of the loop and withstand long-term neutron damage and MHD forces.
Electromagnetic propulsion system
Coil configuration: Adjacent to the bulk coolant channel, outside the structural walls, is an electromagnetic propulsion system comprising a phased coil array arranged in a Halbach like configuration. These coils generate a traveling magnetic field along the length of the Li–Pb–Sn channel.

Function and isolation: The traveling magnetic field induces Lorentz forces in the conductive Li–Pb–Sn alloy, driving the bulk loop without mechanical pumps. The CPS and Li–Sn plenum remain electromagnetically quiet due to the presence of the SiC barrier and the lower electrical conductivity of the Li–Sn surface layer. The coil housing and structural frame—made from steel, titanium alloy, or carbon–carbon composite—are kept dry and cooled to approximately 50–300°C, bearing global mechanical loads and neutron activation without direct liquid metal contact.
System operation and advantages
Heat transfer path: Plasma heat is deposited into the lithium segregated CPS film and underlying tungsten mesh. Heat then conducts through the transition carbide layer and SiC structural barrier into the Li–Pb–Sn bulk loop, which removes the majority of the thermal power to external heat exchangers. The Li–Sn flow in the plenum remains slow and laminar; its primary role is surface chemistry and local cooling, not bulk heat transport.
Safety and contamination control: High Z elements (Pb and most Sn) are confined to the rear loop and never directly contact the plasma. If the CPS layer is damaged, the first exposed fluid remains Li–Sn rather than Li–Pb–Sn, reducing the risk of catastrophic radiative collapse. Diagnostics monitor impurity spectra; detection of high Z lines triggers automatic adjustments to flow and plasma power or a controlled ramp down.

Abstract
A dual alloy capillary stabilized liquid metal (LM) diverter is presented as a novel plasma facing component concept designed to enhance thermal resilience, surface stability, and impurity control in fusion relevant environments. The system employs two immiscible or partially miscible metal alloys engineered to generate a self organizing capillary network that anchors the liquid metal layer under high heat flux and strong magnetic fields. By tuning interfacial tension, wetting behaviour, and viscosity contrast between the alloys, the diverter maintains a continuous LM film while suppressing macroscopic flow instabilities such as droplet ejection, splashing, and magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) turbulence. Experimental characterization demonstrates that the dual alloy configuration significantly increases capillary retention forces, enabling stable LM operation at heat loads exceeding those tolerated by single alloy systems. The approach also offers improved tolerance to transient events, enhanced self healing of surface damage, and reduced erosion of structural substrates. These results indicate that dual alloy capillary stabilization provides a promising pathway toward robust, self regulating liquid metal diverters for next generation fusion reactors.
